How to Calculate Liquidation Price

The formula for a leveraged crypto position, a worked example with maintenance margin and funding, and the parts a simple formula leaves out.

The liquidation price is the price at which an exchange force-closes your leveraged position because the margin left in it has fallen to the maintenance requirement. It is not the price at which your margin hits zero, and it is not simply the entry price minus 1/leverage. This guide shows the formula first, then a full example that includes the costs most short formulas skip.

What the liquidation price means

When you open a leveraged position you post initial margin. As the price moves against you, the unrealized loss eats into that margin. The exchange does not wait for it to reach zero: once the remaining margin falls to the maintenance margin requirement, its liquidation engine starts closing the position. The price at which that happens is the liquidation price.

Exchanges generally compare it against the mark price, not the last traded price, so a short wick on the chart does not necessarily liquidate you. Check which price your exchange uses; this calculator assumes the mark price.

The basic formula

For an isolated-margin, USDT-margined perpetual, ignoring fees and funding, with leverage L and maintenance margin rate MMR as a decimal (0.65% is 0.0065):

  • Long: Liquidation price = Entry × (1 − 1/L + MMR)
  • Short: Liquidation price = Entry × (1 + 1/L − MMR)

The 1/L term is your initial margin as a share of the position. The MMR term takes part of that away: liquidation starts while an MMR-sized slice of margin is still left, so the room you can actually use is 1/L − MMR.

At 10x with a 0.5% maintenance margin rate the usable distance is 10% − 0.5% = 9.5%. At 75x with 0.65% it is 1.333% − 0.65% = 0.683%, about half of what 1/leverage alone suggests.

Adding fees and funding

Two more costs shrink the distance, and both depend on your exchange and how long you hold:

  • Funding. Each funding payment moves margin out of your position (longs when the rate is positive) or into it (shorts when the rate is positive). Over n payments at rate f, a long loses n × f of distance and a short gains it. See why funding fees can eat your buffer.
  • Fees. Some exchanges build the closing fee into the liquidation condition, which moves the trigger closer to entry. Others deduct it only after liquidation has started, which leaves the trigger where it was. Where the fee is in the trigger, this calculator subtracts the opening and the closing fee, so 2 × taker fee. The liquidation fees guide shows how each exchange handles it.

Put together, with the distance as a share of the entry price:

Buffer = 1/L − MMR − fees (only if in the trigger) − funding (long) or + funding (short)

Long: Entry × (1 − Buffer) and Short: Entry × (1 + Buffer)

A worked example

A long position on BTCUSDT with these inputs:

  • Entry price $62,000, margin $5,000, leverage 75x, so a position of $375,000
  • Maintenance margin rate 0.65%
  • Funding 0.01% per 8 hours, held for 24 hours, so 3 payments
  • Taker fee 0.05%, deducted after the trigger (as on Binance in isolated mode)
1 / leverage (1 / 75)1.333%
− maintenance margin rate−0.650%
− funding (3 × 0.01%, long)−0.030%
= buffer0.653%
Liquidation price: $62,000 × (1 − 0.00653)$61,594.93

Ignoring maintenance margin and funding would put the liquidation price at $61,173.33. That is $421.60 further away than the real trigger. The same position as a short would have a buffer of 0.713%, because it receives the funding payments, with a liquidation price of $62,442.27.

What simple formulas leave out

  • Tiered maintenance margin. The rate depends on position size. Binance's tables list a "maintenance amount" per tier, and Bybit subtracts a "maintenance margin deduction", so the requirement is position value × MMR − deduction rather than a flat percentage.
  • Cross margin. The liquidation price depends on your whole account, so one position's numbers cannot give it. Calculators like this one model isolated margin only. See cross margin vs. isolated margin.
  • Slippage. In a fast market the forced close can fill beyond the liquidation price. Liquidation price vs. bankruptcy price explains who absorbs that gap.
  • Partial liquidation. Some exchanges close very large positions in steps rather than all at once.

FAQ

Is the liquidation price the same as the bankruptcy price?

No. The bankruptcy price is where your margin would be exactly zero. The liquidation price comes earlier, when the margin falls to the maintenance requirement, and the difference is what pays for the forced close.

Why does my exchange show a different liquidation price than this formula?

Usually because of tiered maintenance margin (the maintenance amount or deduction), fees that the exchange builds into the trigger, cross margin using the rest of your account, or other open positions and unrealized profit. Treat any formula as an estimate and check the figure your exchange displays.

Does a higher maintenance margin rate move the liquidation price closer?

Yes. The maintenance margin rate is subtracted from the distance, so a higher rate leaves less room. Larger positions fall into higher tiers, which is why the same leverage is riskier at a larger size.

Is liquidation based on the last price or the mark price?

Most exchanges use the mark price, which is derived from an index of several markets, so that a single spike does not liquidate positions. Look up the rule for your exchange and contract.
